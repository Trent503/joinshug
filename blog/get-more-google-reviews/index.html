How to get more Google reviews without chasing customers

You already know you should ask. The reason it does not happen is not discipline — it is that the moment that converts is the moment you are least available. Here is the fix.

Why reviews decide who gets called

A homeowner comparing three contractors cannot evaluate any of them. They do not know what good flashing looks like. They cannot tell a proper repipe from a bad one. They have no way to judge the actual work, which is the only thing you have spent years getting good at.

So they use the proxy available to them. Star rating, review count, and how recent the reviews are.

That is worth sitting with for a second, because it is genuinely unfair and it is also completely fixed. The best operator in your county loses to the third-best one with four times the reviews. Not because homeowners are stupid — because reviews are the only quality signal they can actually read.

It compounds twice over. Review count and recency are inputs to your Google Business Profile ranking, so more reviews means you show up higher in the map pack, which means more people see you, which means more reviews. And if you run Local Services Ads, your review profile influences your lead cost — so the same asset that gets you found also makes your advertising cheaper.

The real problem is not that you forget

Every trade owner knows they should ask for reviews. This is not new information to anyone.

The reason it does not happen is not ignorance and it is not laziness. It is that asking is a task that lives at the end of the day, when the job is done and you are driving home, and the honest competition for that moment is dinner. So it does not get done on Monday, and Tuesday's job stacks on top, and by Friday the Monday customer is a week cold and asking feels weird.

There is a second reason nobody says out loud: asking feels like begging. You did the work, they paid, the transaction is closed, and now you want a favor. That friction is small but it is real, and small friction applied daily wins.

So the answer is not discipline. Discipline has already lost this fight in every trade business I have seen. The answer is removing yourself from the loop entirely.

Timing beats persuasion

Most of the advice about review requests focuses on wording. The wording barely matters. The timing is nearly everything.

There is a window, and it is short. It opens when the customer sees the finished work and closes within about a day. In that window they are as happy with you as they will ever be — the problem is gone, the house looks better, the relief is fresh. Ask then and a large share of people say yes.

Wait a week and the feeling has flattened into normal. The clean driveway is just the driveway now. The working furnace is just the furnace. Nothing went wrong, so nothing is top of mind, and the same request that would have converted on Tuesday gets ignored on the following Monday.

This is why manual asking fails even when people are disciplined about it. The moment you need to hit is the moment you are least available — you are packing up, driving to the next job, or already home.

How to ask, in one text

A text, not an email. Email review requests get opened at a fraction of the rate and half of them land in promotions.

Short. Two sentences at most. Anything longer reads like marketing and gets swiped.

A direct link straight to the review form. Not to your website, not to your profile — to the box where they type. Every extra tap between the text and the typing loses people, and "search for us on Google" loses almost all of them.

Personal, from you, from a number they recognize as the business they just dealt with.

And one ask. If they do not respond, that is the end of it. A second nudge a few days later is defensible. A third is harassment and it is a way to turn a neutral customer into a negative review.

Roughly: Thanks for having us out today — glad the roof is sorted. If you have thirty seconds, a quick Google review helps us a lot: [link]

That is the whole thing. There is no clever version that outperforms it.

Why review gating will cost you everything

Review gating is when you survey customers first and only send the Google link to the happy ones. Plenty of software sells this as a feature. It is worth being blunt about it.

It violates Google's policies. It is explicitly against the rules, not a gray area. And the consequence is not a warning — profiles caught doing it can have reviews removed in bulk, and a business can lose years of accumulated proof in an afternoon.

The same applies to buying reviews, incentivizing them with discounts, or asking staff and family to leave them. Google is measurably better at detecting these than it was five years ago, and the downside is catastrophic while the upside is a temporary bump.

Ask everybody. If your work is good, the average takes care of itself. If your work is not good, no review strategy fixes that and you have a different problem to solve first.

What to do about the bad one

You will get one. Everyone does, and a perfect five-star profile with sixty reviews actually reads as suspicious to a lot of people.

Respond to it. Publicly, quickly, and without arguing. Not for the person who left it — they are gone. For the next forty homeowners who read it while deciding whether to call you.

What that reader is looking for is whether you are the kind of operation that handles a problem like an adult. A calm, specific, non-defensive reply that acknowledges the issue and states what you did about it converts better than the complaint costs you. A defensive reply blaming the customer costs you far more than the original review ever would have.

The volume argument matters here too. One negative review inside a hundred is noise. Inside eight, it is the headline. The best defense against a bad review is a steady flow of ordinary good ones — which is an argument for automating the ask rather than for managing your reputation after the fact.

Making it a system instead of a chore

The version that works has you doing nothing.

Connect the request to job completion in the software you already run. If you use Jobber or Housecall Pro, closing the job is already a thing you do — so the review request fires off that action, in the window that matters, without anyone remembering anything.

No dashboard to check. No weekly review-chasing session that gets skipped. No decision about whether this particular customer seemed happy enough, which is both the gateway to gating and a judgment you are bad at making about your own work.

Then it compounds quietly. Ten jobs a week at even a 25 percent response rate is over a hundred new reviews a year, arriving steadily, which keeps the recency signal fresh in a way a one-time push never does.

That is why the review automation is included at every tier rather than sold as an add-on. It is not a nice-to-have layered on top of local SEO — it is half of local SEO, and it is the half most operators never get to.

You already earned the reputation. This is just about making sure it shows up where people are looking.
