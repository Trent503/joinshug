What a trades website actually needs to book jobs

Most trade websites were built to be looked at. Yours needs to be built to be used. Here is the anatomy of one that books jobs, and a checklist to run against yours.

The one job the site has to do

Your website has exactly one job. Turn a stranger who is already looking for what you do into a phone call.

That is it. It is not there to look impressive. It is not a brochure. It is not a place to put your mission statement. Every element on it either moves someone toward calling you or it is taking up space that something useful could occupy.

This sounds obvious and almost no trade website is built this way. Most were built to be approved by the owner, not to be used by a homeowner. Those are different tests. The owner looks at it on a desktop monitor, scrolls the whole thing, and asks whether it looks professional. The homeowner looks at it on a cracked phone screen, standing in the driveway looking at the problem, and asks one question: can this person fix my thing.

Build for the second person.

What has to be above the fold

Above the fold means what they see before scrolling, on a phone. That is roughly six inches of screen. It has to carry four things.

What you do, in their words. Not "premium exterior solutions." Roof repair. Drain cleaning. AC replacement. Use the words they typed into Google, because the match between what they searched and what they see is what stops the back button.

Where you do it. Service area named, visibly. A huge share of bounces are people who could not tell in three seconds whether you cover them.

How to reach you. A tappable phone number. Not text in an image. Not a number they have to scroll to find. On mobile it should be pinned in the header so it is reachable at every scroll position.

One reason to believe you. Your Google rating, years in business, license number, whichever is strongest. One. Not a badge farm.

If those four are not there, nothing further down the page matters, because most people never get further down the page.

Speed is a conversion feature, not a vanity metric

Page speed gets talked about as an SEO thing. It is, but that undersells it. It is a conversion thing first.

Someone searching for a plumber at 9pm with water on the floor has the patience of a hummingbird. Every second of load time bleeds a percentage of them, and the bleeding is worst on exactly the traffic you want most — the urgent traffic, on cell service, in a hurry.

Most trade sites are slow for the same reason. They were built on a page builder with a stack of plugins, a slider, a font library, a chat widget, an analytics tag, a pixel, and four megabytes of uncompressed photos from someone's phone.

The fix is unglamorous. Compress the images and size them properly. Drop the slider — nobody has ever hired a contractor because of a slider. Drop the chat widget unless a human is genuinely answering it. Keep the page a page instead of an application.

Test it yourself: open your site on your phone, on cell data, not on the shop wifi. Count. If you get past three seconds before the phone number is visible, that is your highest-return fix and it is not close.

Proof that actually convinces a homeowner

There is a hierarchy here and most sites lead with the weakest item on it.

Your own photos, of your own work. This is the strongest thing you have and it costs nothing. Your truck. Your crew. Finished jobs. Before-and-afters. Ten real photos beat a hundred stock images, and homeowners can tell the difference instantly even if they could not tell you how.

Google reviews, shown with the count. Not hand-picked quotes in a slider with no source. The rating and the number, pulled from the profile they can go verify. A quote with a first name and no link reads as invented, because most of them are.

License and insurance, stated plainly. Numbers where applicable. This filters you into the "real company" bucket before anyone consciously thinks about it.

Specifics about the work. The materials you install, the process you follow, what a typical job actually involves. Specificity reads as competence. Vagueness reads as a middleman.

What does not work: stock photography of models in clean uniforms, testimonials with no attribution, badge walls of associations nobody has heard of, and any number you cannot substantiate. Homeowners are more skeptical than trade owners think, and a claim that feels inflated poisons the ones that are true.

The form is where most sites bleed out

Here is the pattern I see constantly. Everything upstream works. The site loads, the copy is right, the photos are good. Then the contact form asks for eleven things.

Name, email, phone, address, city, zip, service type, preferred date, preferred time, how did you hear about us, and a message box marked required. Every one of those fields is a place someone quits, and the ones near the end are quit by people who had already decided to hire you.

Ask for a name and a phone number. That is enough to call somebody. You can get the address on the phone, where it takes four seconds and no willpower.

Some other things that matter more than they should:

  • Real labels on the fields, not just placeholder text that vanishes when they start typing
  • Correct input types, so the phone field opens the number pad instead of the alphabet keyboard
  • Autocomplete attributes, so their phone fills it in for them — this alone measurably lifts mobile completion
  • An error message that says what is wrong, in a place they can see, not a red outline with no explanation
  • A success state that replaces the form in place, so they know it went through

And make the phone number at least as prominent as the form. A big share of trade customers will never fill out a form under any circumstances. They want to talk to a person.

What happens after they hit submit

This is the part that is not really the website, and it is the part that decides whether the website was worth building.

A form that emails an inbox you check on Sundays is not a lead system. It is a way to find out about jobs you lost. The submission needs to text you immediately, with the name and the number, so you can call back while they are still sitting there.

Speed to lead is the least glamorous advantage in this business and one of the most reliable. The operator who calls back in five minutes beats the one who calls back in five hours nearly every time, regardless of who is better at the actual work. The homeowner is not evaluating craftsmanship at that stage. They are evaluating whether anyone is going to help them.

And when they call you instead and you cannot pick up — because you are on a roof, which is where you should be — that call needs to be caught. An automatic text back within seconds, while they still have the phone in their hand, is the difference between a job and a job your competitor booked. The arithmetic on that leak is worth reading, because the number is bigger than most owners guess.

The checklist

Open your site on your phone right now, on cell data, and go down this list honestly.

  • Is the phone number tappable and visible without scrolling?
  • Does the first screen say what you do, in the words a customer would use?
  • Is your service area named where they can see it?
  • Does it load before you get bored waiting?
  • Are the photos yours?
  • Is your Google rating and review count shown?
  • Does the form ask for two things or eleven?
  • Does a submission text you immediately?
  • Does a missed call get texted back automatically?
  • Is there a page for each service you actually want more of?

Most sites fail four or more of those. The good news is that every one of them is fixable, and the first three are usually a single afternoon's work with whoever built the thing.
