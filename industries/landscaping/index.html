Landscaping

The spring rush decides your year. It also arrives while you're on a mower.

An AI receptionist for landscaping and lawn care businesses. Shug answers every call, tells a recurring maintenance enquiry from a one-off install, captures the property details, and books the estimate.

What it does for a landscaping company: answers 24/7 through the spring surge, separates recurring maintenance contracts from one-off design and install work, captures lot size and access, books estimates, and keeps existing contract customers from getting lost behind new enquiries.

Two completely different businesses share one phone number

Landscaping is unusual in that the same phone line receives two kinds of call with almost nothing in common.

One is recurring maintenance: mowing, edging, seasonal clean-ups, a contract worth a modest amount every month for years. The other is design and install: a patio, a retaining wall, an irrigation system — a large one-off with a long sales cycle and a real estimate behind it.

They need different questions, different urgency, and different follow-up. Handled identically, one of them always suffers. In practice it is usually the recurring contract that gets neglected, because the install has a bigger number attached — even though the contract is the thing that pays through winter.

What Shug asks a landscaping caller

  • Recurring or one-off. The first fork in the conversation, and everything downstream depends on it.
  • Roughly how big the property is. Lot size or something concrete the homeowner can actually answer.
  • Access. Gate width, slope, and whether a machine can get to the back. The questions that decide whether a quote survives contact with the site.
  • What they want done, in their words. Captured as said rather than forced into a category, because homeowners describe landscaping loosely and the detail matters.
  • Timeline. A patio wanted before a graduation party is a different job from one wanted eventually.
  • Existing customer or new. So the people already paying you monthly do not sit behind first-time callers in April.

April is not a month, it's an event

The first stretch of warm weather sets off every homeowner who has been looking at their garden through a window since November.

Those calls arrive in a compressed window, while your crews are at maximum load and you personally are on a machine. It is the single worst combination of high demand and low availability in the trade calendar, and it is when annual maintenance contracts are won or lost — because whoever answers in April holds that customer for the next several years.

Winter, and the quiet-season problem

The mirror image is just as costly. In the quiet months the phone rings rarely enough that it stops being watched carefully, and a snow removal call or an early design enquiry slips.

Shug answers with the same consistency in January as in April. That matters more than it sounds, because off-season design enquiries are often the largest jobs of the following year.

Questions from this trade

Can it tell a mowing contract from a patio install?

Yes, and that is the first thing it establishes. Recurring maintenance and one-off design or install work need different questions and different follow-up, so Shug forks the conversation early and captures what each one actually needs.

Will my existing contract customers get lost in the spring rush?

Not if you set it up that way. Shug can ask whether the caller is already a customer and route accordingly, so the people paying you every month are not queued behind first-time enquiries in April.

Does it work in the off-season, when the phone barely rings?

Yes, and that is quietly one of the better reasons to have it. Off-season design enquiries are often the largest jobs of the following year, and they are the ones most likely to slip when nobody is watching the phone closely.
