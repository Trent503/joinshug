HVAC demand is a weather forecast, not a schedule

Two or three times a year, the weather decides your week for you.

The first genuinely cold night in autumn generates more no-heat calls in twelve hours than the previous month combined. The first stretch above ninety does the same for cooling. Everybody who has been ignoring a marginal system calls at once, and they all call in the same four-hour window — usually the evening, because that is when they noticed.

You cannot hire for that curve and you cannot answer it from inside an attic. This is the clearest case in the trades for a phone that scales to zero and to forty without you touching it.

What Shug asks an HVAC caller

  • Heat or cooling, and is it running at all. Not working and not turning on are different trucks.
  • What the thermostat says versus what the house feels like. The fastest way to separate a control problem from an equipment problem.
  • Any water, ice, or unusual smell. The three things that change a routine call into one you may want to hear about now.
  • Age and type of the system. Furnace, heat pump, package unit, mini-split — and roughly how old, which frames repair against replace before you arrive.
  • Is anyone in the house especially vulnerable. Infants, elderly residents, or someone with a medical need. This is the transfer rule most HVAC companies want and most answering services never ask about.
  • Are they on a maintenance plan. So your existing customers get sorted ahead of cold callers during a surge, which is the whole point of selling the plan.

The vulnerable-household rule

Of everything on this page, this is the one worth setting up carefully.

A house with no heat at 20 degrees and an infant in it is a different call from a house with no heat and two adults who own sweaters. Most HVAC owners already make that judgement instinctively, and most call-handling systems have no way to capture it. Shug can ask, and you can make it an automatic transfer.

It is also the answer to the objection that AI cannot exercise judgement. It cannot — but you can, once, at setup, and then it applies consistently at 2am when a tired human would not have thought to ask.

Maintenance plans and the quiet months

The other half of an HVAC phone is not emergencies at all. It is renewals, tune-up scheduling, filter questions, and the estimate calls that turn into system replacements in the shoulder seasons.

Those calls are lower drama and higher lifetime value, and they are exactly the calls that get lost when a business is in surge mode. Shug handles them at the same standard in February as in July, because it is not tired in either month.

Questions from this trade

Can it cope with a hundred calls on the first cold night?

That is the scenario an AI receptionist is best suited to, because callers are not queued behind a single person answering a phone. Every caller gets answered, triaged and captured, and you work the list in the order your rules define rather than the order they happened to ring.

Can it prioritise a house with no heat and a newborn in it?

Yes, if you set it up that way. Shug can ask whether anyone in the household is especially vulnerable and treat that as an immediate transfer. It is one of the most useful rules an HVAC company can define during setup.

Will existing maintenance-plan customers get treated differently?

They can. Shug can ask whether the caller is on a plan and route accordingly, so the customers who pay you every year are not stuck behind first-time callers during a surge.
